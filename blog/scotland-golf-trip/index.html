Birdies, Bogeys, and Rolling Hills: What Every Lady Golfer Should Know Before Planning a Golf Trip to Scotland

Thinking about golfing in Scotland? Join Renetta and Erin as they share what they learned golfing in the Home of Golf — from navigating historic courses and unpredictable weather to packing the right gear, understanding golf etiquette, and building friendships along the way.

Renetta Smith and Erin Stowell · August 10, 2026 ·

Did you know that Scotland is known as "the home of golf"? Yup. While the exact origins of golf remain a topic of friendly debate among die-hard golfers, Scotland is where the game evolved into the sport we know and love today. Some say Musselburgh Links in East Lothian, one of the world's oldest golf courses still in play, is where golf originated. Others point to the Old Course at St Andrews in Fife, often regarded as the mecca of golf. And then there's Leith Links in Edinburgh, where the earliest known written rules of golf were signed by John Rattay in 1744.

Historically, golf developed largely as a gentleman's game. In 1744, a group of prominent golfers in Edinburgh organized the Company of Gentlemen Golfers — later known as the Honourable Company of Edinburgh Golfers — and established 13 written rules for a competition at Leith Links. But women were playing too. In fact, the first formal documented reference to women playing golf dates to 1738 at Bruntsfield Links in Edinburgh.

Fast-forward a few centuries, and women from around the world now travel to Scotland to experience golf in its historic home. But as a woman golfer of color, I had a question that a website or travel blog couldn't answer for me: How welcome would I actually feel playing golf in Scotland?

This post is my honest answer to that question — and everything else I wish someone had told me before I booked the trip.

My daughter attended university in Edinburgh and has lived there for several years, so I travel to Scotland frequently to visit her and my grand-cat, Iphigenia. During my visit in December 2025, I began my quest to visit golf courses throughout Edinburgh.

What to Know Before You Go

Scotland's golf culture is steeped in tradition — which is both its greatest charm and its steepest learning curve for visitors. Here's what we learned:

The Courses

Scottish links courses are unlike anything you'll play in the United States. They're carved into coastal land, exposed to wind and weather, with undulating fairways and firm, fast greens. Unlike many courses in the US with manicured fairways, man-made lakes, and engineered hazards, Scottish courses feel as though the game was built around the natural landscape — beautifully maintained grasslands, naturally contoured terrain, and some of the most breathtaking scenery you'll ever see on a golf course. Don't expect manicured perfection — expect raw beauty and a genuine test of your game.

  • Book tee times well in advance — especially for iconic courses like St Andrews. Some require ballot entries months ahead.
  • Walk the course if you can. Most traditional Scottish courses are walking-only or strongly encourage it. Bring a trolley or hire a caddie.
  • Respect the pace of play. Scottish golfers take pace seriously. Know your handicap, be ready to play, and keep up with the group ahead.

The Weather

Pack for all four seasons — in a single day. Scotland's weather is famously unpredictable. I have visited Scotland in summer and winter, and regardless of the time of year, I've learned to be prepared for cool temperatures, brief rain showers, and high winds. We played in sunshine, wind, mist, and sideways rain all within the same round. And honestly? That's part of the magic.

  • Waterproof everything: jacket, trousers, gloves, bag cover.
  • Layers are non-negotiable. You may start out comfortable, become warm climbing hills, hit a rain shower, and then be cold when the wind picks up — all in one round.
  • Wind-resistant clothing performs better than just warm clothing.
  • Consider a fitted, waterproof beanie instead of relying on a golf cap or visor. High winds can make traditional golf hats more trouble than they're worth.

The People

Here's my honest answer to the question I couldn't find in any travel blog: we felt welcome. Not performatively welcome — genuinely welcomed. Scots are very friendly people. They are respectful to those who are kind and respectful to them, their traditions, and their property. And they are proud of their country and its history. Being from the USA — or any other country — does not make you or your traditions better. It only makes them different. Respect their country, their people, their property, and their history, and you'll find the golf community in Scotland to be wonderfully inclusive.

We encountered warmth at every course, in every clubhouse, from every caddie. I've had the wonderful opportunity to meet and golf with Scottish lady golfers who were welcoming, patient, and great teachers when it came to reading and navigating each course.

We didn't just play golf in Scotland. We connected — with history, with the land, and with each other.

Etiquette Notes for Lady Golfers

  • Dress codes are enforced at most clubs — call ahead to confirm requirements. Collared shirts, tailored trousers or skirts/shorts of appropriate length are standard.
  • Know the rules of golf and golf etiquette. Whether playing a social or competitive round, understanding pace of play, remaining silent when another golfer is addressing the ball, and proper course care are non-negotiable.
  • Repair your divots and pitch marks. Every time.
  • Turn off or silence your phone on the course.
  • A friendly hello on the first tee goes a long way.
  • Don't assume the golf pro shop will be open. Call ahead to confirm hours if buying course merchandise or souvenirs is important to you. Arrive with the golf balls and tees you need.

Preparing for Your Trip

Beyond knowing golf etiquette, there are some very practical things I've learned about preparing for a golf trip to Scotland that I wish someone had told me before my first trip.

Shipping Your Clubs

Consider shipping your golf clubs instead of flying with them. For the ease of international travel, I use a door-to-door shipping service that specializes in golf equipment. For domestic travel, I generally schedule pickup about a week before departure; for international trips, I allow approximately two to three weeks. Selecting a shipper you trust is important, but so is properly preparing your clubs for travel. Check with your shipping company for prohibited items and packing requirements. I leave my club heads attached, wrap each club individually in bubble wrap, and pack everything snugly in a hard-sided travel case.

I schedule and pay for shipping online and arrange for the carrier to pick up my clubs. I never leave them unattended outside for pickup — if they're being collected from my home, I leave a note asking the driver to ring the doorbell so I can hand over the case personally.

I also pack one pair of golf shoes — with socks tucked inside — and a few lightweight accessories such as golf balls, tees, and ball markers in the case.

Gear & Packing Tips

  • Choose a lightweight golf bag that fits a standard push cart or electric trolley. I purchased a full-size MacGregor cart bag, and for Scotland, that was a mistake. It was heavier than I needed, and maneuvering that combination up and down Scotland's hills — especially in the wind and rain — was exhausting. A lightweight Sunday bag or a compact stand bag is far more practical.
  • You don't necessarily need a full set of clubs. For some of the casual and social rounds I played, I could have gotten by with five to seven clubs. Save your back and pack smart.
  • Learn how to operate an electric trolley before you take one onto the course. The electric trolleys I encountered in Scotland are similar to those used in the US, but I wish I'd rented one beforehand to learn the basics: how to install and remove the battery, how to fold and unfold the cart, and how to maneuver it on uneven terrain.
  • Wear comfortable, waterproof golf shoes with good traction. Your shoes need to handle uneven terrain in both wet and dry conditions — and, based on my experience, prepare for wet.
  • Keep your rain gear lightweight. A full-size umbrella and heavy rain pants were more gear than I wanted to haul around the course. A lightweight waterproof jacket that folds into your bag is far more practical.
  • Ladies' golf apparel — especially plus-size apparel — can be difficult to find. During my visits to golf shops around Edinburgh, I found considerably more golf apparel for men than for women, and women's selections tended to stop at smaller sizes. If you're plus-size or curvy, bring the apparel you need with you.
  • Pack only what you need. A few things I was glad I had: a small battery-operated tabletop fan, socks for sleeping, hand warmers, and warmer pajamas. You don't have to bring everything from home — many online retailers operate UK-specific websites and can deliver purchases directly to your destination.

My Golf Course Experience: Lessons Learned

Golfing in Scotland has challenged me in ways I didn't expect — from learning to navigate unfamiliar courses and unpredictable weather to discovering just how heavy a golf bag can feel when you're climbing a Scottish hill. But those challenges have also been part of what has made the experiences memorable. Here's what I learned on the course.

  • You will likely be walking the course — so be prepared. Depending on the course, you'll either carry your golf bag or use a manual or electric golf trolley. Golf buggies (carts) may be restricted or unavailable on some courses. Check with the course before you arrive.
  • Prepare your body, not just your gear. Scottish golf courses can be physically demanding. Hills, uneven terrain, and unpredictable weather will test your endurance. Walking 18 holes on a windy links course is a workout.
  • Accommodations may differ from what you're accustomed to in the US. Many vacation rentals are smaller, may not have air conditioning, and can have limited storage. Heating is often provided by radiators. You may find twin or double beds rather than the larger beds common in American hotels.
  • Scotland's infrastructure is different. Many streets are cobblestoned, hilly, and narrow. Cars are typically smaller. The cities are very walkable for sightseeing, and public transportation — including taxis, trams, and buses — is readily available. But access for larger vehicles may be limited in some areas.
Key Takeaways
  • Ship your golf clubs well in advance of your departure if you choose not to fly with them.
  • Scottish golf courses can be physically demanding. Prepare your gear — and your body.
  • Keep your golf gear lightweight.
  • If you're plus-size or curvy, bring the golf apparel you need with you.

What We'd Tell Every Lady Golfer Planning This Trip

Go. Full stop. Don't wait until your game is "good enough." Don't wait for the perfect travel companion. Don't wait for the right season.

Go for the history. Go for the courses. Go for the people. Go for the stories you'll tell for the rest of your life about the round you played in the rain at a links course that's been in play for three centuries.

If playing golf in Scotland is somewhere on your bucket list, don't wait until you feel completely prepared. Do your homework, pack wisely, respect the game and the country you're visiting — and go experience it for yourself. You don't have to be a low-scoring golfer, but you must respect the course, the culture, and the people you encounter.

And go knowing that you belong on every course you step onto — in Scotland or anywhere else.

Trip Planning Resources

Visit Scotland Golf (visitscotland.com) has a comprehensive guide to courses, regions, and travel planning. For St Andrews tee times, book directly through the St Andrews Links Trust at standrews.com. And if you're traveling with a group of lady golfers, consider contacting courses directly — many offer group packages and warm welcomes for visiting women's groups.
