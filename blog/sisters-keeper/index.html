Am I My Sister's Keeper?

What does it really mean to be our sister's keeper? In this honest reflection on sisterhood, friendship, discernment, and boundaries, Renetta explores the difference between caring for another woman and becoming responsible for her choices. It's a conversation about extending grace without abandoning accountability, offering support without over-functioning, and recognizing that forgiveness doesn't always require reconciliation — or restored access. Healthy sisterhood can hold both compassion and boundaries.

Renetta Smith, Founder & CEO, no more hiding™ · August 28, 2026 ·

Yes, I am my sister's keeper, but what I had to learn was where my sisterly responsibility ends and my sister's personal responsibility begins.

Sisterhood and friendship aren't the same thing. It took me 50 years to learn the difference and how to position these two relational concepts to establish boundaries and restore my peace. Embracing the distinction taught me to reconsider what — and how much — I owed to each relationship. It allowed me to forgive and heal while simultaneously deciding how much physical and emotional access I allowed others to have to me.

Sisterhood is a broad, often unspoken sense of connection, responsibility, or solidarity among women. But it can also carry an expectation that because we are women, we are somehow obligated to support, rescue, defend, accommodate, or remain loyal to one another — sometimes at the expense of our own discernment. With sisterhood, the commitment is much more fundamental: I see you. I respect you. I won't degrade your character. I don't have to compete with you simply because you're another woman. When appropriate and reasonable, I may extend a hand.

Friendship is a personal, reciprocal relationship that is tested and proven over time as greater levels of trust and emotional intimacy are earned. With friendship, there may reasonably be greater expectations: checking in, emotional availability, keeping confidences, showing up during difficult times, investing in the relationship, and repairing disagreements.

You choose your friends based on things like trust, compatibility, shared experiences, affection, reciprocity, discretion, and demonstrated character. Friendship develops over time, and greater intimacy is earned. Sisterhood doesn't require a personal relationship. It starts with recognizing another woman as a fellow woman worthy of dignity, respect, consideration, and perhaps a reasonable degree of support.

Sisterhood alone does not entitle another woman to the privileges of friendship.

And get this, Sis: you can graduate from sister to friend and later be repositioned based on repeated behaviors that violate your trust. Because access isn't permanent simply because somebody once earned it. A woman can move from sister to friend when, over time, she demonstrates trustworthiness, reciprocity, discretion, consistency, and character. And she can absolutely move from friend back to sister when repeated behavior demonstrates that the level of access she once had is no longer appropriate.

The important part is that repositioning doesn't require hatred. You don't have to become enemies. You don't have to expose her, retaliate, gossip about her, or conduct a ceremonial friendship funeral. You can simply reposition the relationship.

Some relationships don't need to end; they need to be repositioned.

And there's another layer here: healthy friendship has room for mistakes, apologies, repair, growth, and grace. I'm specifically talking about patterns — repeated offenses, repeated indiscretion, repeated boundary violations, repeated one-sidedness, and repeated demonstrations that someone cannot responsibly manage the access she's been given. That's discernment, not punishment.

So maybe the question isn't simply, "Am I my sister's keeper?"

Maybe we first need to ask:

What does being my sister's keeper actually require of me?

What Does Sisterly Commitment Actually Require?

Because now we're not talking about cutting everybody off every time they disappoint us. That's not discernment either. We're talking about paying attention to what the relationship has shown us. It means recognizing that women are human and that loving one another well does not require perfection.

But neither does forgiveness require restored access.

Remember This

Forgiveness can restore peace without restoring access.

You can forgive Sis.

You can stop being angry with Sis.

You can genuinely wish Sis well.

You can even continue interacting with Sis.

And still decide Sis doesn't get the password to your life anymore.

Because forgiveness, reconciliation, trust, and access are separate decisions. Forgiveness is something I can choose for my own peace. Reconciliation requires participation from both of us. Trust has to be rebuilt through demonstrated behavior. And access? Access is mine to give. That doesn't mean I'm punishing another woman. Discernment responds to evidence.

I Can Be My Sister's Keeper Without Becoming Her Caretaker

I believe I have a responsibility toward my sisters. However, I no longer believe that responsibility requires me to take responsibility for my sisters.

Now here's where this conversation gets a little uncomfortable, because this is an area where I've struggled personally. I've wanted more for some of my sisters than they wanted for themselves. I could clearly see their potential. I could see possibilities for their lives that they said they passionately wanted for themselves. But somewhere along the way, I failed to recognize when their words and their actions weren't aligned. And instead of allowing that disconnect to tell me something, I would work harder. I would encourage more. Research more. Help more. Plan more. Until eventually, without even realizing it, I was driving someone else's journey. And I had to learn that wanting more for someone doesn't mean I can — or should — take responsibility for getting her there. Because if we're going to talk about our obligation to support our sisters, then we also have to talk about our sisters' obligation to themselves.

Over the years, I've supported women I cared about through difficult seasons — career transitions, unemployment, financial uncertainty, divorces, relocations, and those uncomfortable periods when you simply don't know what to do next to make life make sense again. I've helped develop career plans, updated résumés, researched housing options, shared resources, made recommendations, made introductions, and helped identify next steps. I genuinely wanted to help. But sometimes I would check back months — and even years — later and discover that very little had changed. The plan they asked for and helped create hadn't been worked. The research hadn't been done. The next steps hadn't been taken. And yet, occasionally, the request for help would grow. Not "Can you help me think through my next step?" but "Can you help me figure out my life?" And sometimes the help being asked of me would require far more than support. It would require me to take on responsibility that wasn't mine to carry. That's where I had to learn to ask a question that once made me terribly uncomfortable: "Okay, Sis. What's your plan?"

I eventually learned that doing more for another woman than she was prepared to do for herself wasn't necessarily helping either one of us. Those experiences forced me to ask myself another difficult question: At what point does being my sister's keeper turn into carrying my sister? Because those are not the same thing. I can help you develop a career transition plan. I can encourage you. I can share what I know. I can help you identify resources. I can open a door to opportunity. I can even walk beside you for a while. But, Sis… I cannot walk your part of the journey for you. At some point, you have an obligation to yourself.

That doesn't mean you'll always get it right. It doesn't mean circumstances won't knock you flat. And it certainly doesn't mean you won't need help — sometimes a whole lot of help. We all do. But there is a difference between needing a hand and expecting someone else to take responsibility for your life. And maybe that's another place where we misunderstand sisterhood. Supporting another woman does not require rescuing her. Setting a boundary does not mean I stopped caring about her. Saying "no" does not automatically mean I failed to support another woman. And refusing to assume responsibility for something another grown woman can reasonably do for herself doesn't make me a bad sister.

I can be my sister's keeper without becoming my sister's caretaker.

When Self-Protection Becomes Distrust

After being hurt in friendships with women I trusted, I learned how to protect myself. And some of that protection was necessary, but eventually I had to ask myself: Was I using discernment to decide who deserved access to me — or was I making new women pay the security deposit for damage they didn't cause? This is where I've had to check myself, too.

I got tired of sisterhoods and friendships that felt one-sided. I got tired of being disappointed. I got tired of discovering that women I considered friends didn't necessarily understand friendship the way I did. And I got burned. More than once.

And some of that disappointment was mine to own. I hadn't yet learned how to clearly communicate what I needed from a friendship, establish boundaries, or recognize when a relationship was asking more of me than it was giving back. For a long time, I looked at those experiences mostly in terms of what those women had done to me. But age, distance, therapy, self-work, and a whole lot of reflection have helped me look at some of those relationships differently. Some of those women may not have known how to be friends.

That doesn't excuse hurtful behavior. But it helped me understand something: we talk about friendship and sisterhood as though everybody received the same instruction manual. We didn't. Some women grew up watching healthy female friendships. They watched women disagree respectfully and repair. They saw women celebrate one another without competition. They saw women tell each other the truth, apologize, forgive, establish boundaries, keep confidences, and show up for each other when life got ugly.

Other women never saw any of that.

I was somewhere in between.

Growing up, I didn't know there was a difference between sisterhood and friendship. I grew up with limited resources, and most of the friendships I developed came through classmates and church. My world was relatively small. Outside of school, work, and church, I didn't have much of a social life. The friends I occasionally hung out with were church friends, and we'd meet at Wyatt's Cafeteria, Luby's, and Braum's after morning worship or evening service.

I was an active member of the church community for most of my life, and for a long time, I assumed that the women we called "sisters" at church represented what sisterhood was supposed to look like. Over time, I learned that simply calling one another "sister" didn't necessarily make it sisterhood. And that made me realize something: none of us gets a friendship instruction manual. There's no rulebook handed to us that says:

  • Here's how you become a good friend.
  • Here's what loyalty looks like.
  • Here's the difference between supporting somebody and enabling them.
  • Here's how you disagree without destroying the relationship.
  • Here's how you celebrate another woman when her success makes you question your own life.
  • Here's how you tell your sister the truth when the truth might piss her off.

We bring what we learned — or didn't learn — into our adult friendships. And neither we nor the women we become friends with necessarily realize how much those early experiences shaped what we came to expect from friendship.

And when we get hurt and disappointed enough times, we build emotional walls to protect and defend ourselves. For a while, protection meant keeping people at a distance. I had been disappointed enough times that I became cautious about who I trusted and how much of myself I shared. Some of that was healthy — I needed boundaries. I needed to pay attention to patterns. I needed to stop giving people access to me simply because we grew up together, attended the same church, worked together, or called each other friend. But I also had to be careful not to make every new sister pay for what another sister had done. That's where discernment became important. Discernment allowed me to stop assuming that every woman was either automatically trustworthy or automatically a threat. I could simply pause and pay attention. People will usually show you who they are. My responsibility is to pay attention when they do.

  • What does she show me?
  • Is she consistent?
  • Does she respect my privacy?
  • Can she celebrate me without competing with me?
  • Can we disagree without the relationship becoming ugly?
  • Does she only show up when she needs something?

And just as importantly, what am I showing her? Because I'm not exempt from any of this. Some of us will extend chance after chance to the men in our lives. We'll forgive, explain away behavior, wait for change, and sometimes lower the bar so low it's lying under the floorboards. Yet one painful experience with another woman can make us suspicious of women for years. Why?

I don't know that there is one answer. Maybe it's past betrayal. Maybe competition. Maybe jealousy or insecurity. Maybe it's what we saw growing up. Maybe some of us simply learned that keeping other women at a distance felt safer. Protecting myself doesn't require me to distrust every woman I meet. Nor does sisterhood require me to trust every woman I meet. That's what discernment is for. Every woman may be my sister, but every woman isn't necessarily my friend. And that's okay.

So Where Does Sisterly Commitment Begin — and Where Does It End?

Maybe sisterhood begins with recognizing each other. I see you. I respect your humanity. I will not intentionally tear you down to make myself feel bigger. I can celebrate you without diminishing myself. I can tell you the truth with love. I can refuse to participate if other people are tearing you apart. I can extend a hand when you stumble. I can offer what I reasonably have to give. And when I love you closely enough to call you friend, I can show up with honesty, consistency, accountability, grace, and loyalty.

But sisterhood also needs boundaries because being your keeper cannot require abandoning myself. And being kept cannot mean waiting for another woman to rescue you from responsibilities that belong to you. Maybe healthy sisterhood requires something from both of us. If I extend my hand, will you reach? If I help you create the plan, will you work it?

If I tell you the truth, will you at least consider it before deciding I'm against you? If I establish a boundary, can you respect it without interpreting my "no" as rejection?

And on the other side: If my sister asks for help, will I listen before judging? Will I offer what I reasonably can without keeping score? Can I celebrate another woman without comparing her life to mine? Can I recognize when my past hurt is causing me to distrust a woman who hasn't done anything to me? Can I be supportive without trying to control the outcome? Can I let another woman be different from me without making her wrong?

Those questions are harder than simply declaring, "Women should support women." Because sisterhood requires more than a slogan. It requires discernment. Boundaries. Accountability. Grace. Reciprocity. And sometimes forgiveness. Not necessarily reconciliation or friendship. And certainly not unlimited access.

I've learned that we can also become over-obligated to what I call "the sisterhood." Sometimes "support other women" becomes a kind of moral pressure that tells us a good woman should keep giving, helping, accommodating, forgiving, opening doors, sharing resources, and showing up — even when the relationship itself is telling us to stop.

I used to think, She's my sister. I should help. Now I've learned to ask a few more questions.

  • What has she demonstrated?
  • Is this relationship reciprocal?
  • Is she taking responsibility for herself?
  • Does she value me — or primarily what I can do for her?

And perhaps most importantly: What is my discernment telling me before I say yes?

That doesn't make me hard. It means I've finally learned to use my discernment muscles.

My growth isn't that I stopped caring about other women. It's that I learned caring and discernment have to coexist. Sisterhood should never require us to override our discernment simply to prove our commitment to other women. So maybe the answer to "Am I my sister's keeper?" is yes, but perhaps we've misunderstood what keeping one another means.

Sisterhood requires reciprocity, but reciprocity doesn't mean we always give each other the same thing. It means we each remain responsible for what is ours to carry.

I believe women have a responsibility to care about the wellbeing of other women. But I've learned that caring for my sisters doesn't require carrying them, nor does sisterhood require giving every woman unlimited access to me or my resources. My key life lessons have been:

  1. Clarify the need. Some women just want a listening ear. Others simply want to run an idea by someone with expertise. She's not necessarily in distress, and she doesn't necessarily need saving. Before jumping in to help, I've learned to first understand what she actually needs.
  2. Clarify the help I can provide. In my eagerness to support sisterhood, I've sometimes committed to helping women before understanding how committed they were to helping themselves.
  3. Care wisely. I can remain openhearted while using discernment to understand what sisterhood requires of me — and what it doesn't.
  4. Know what belongs to her. What belongs to another woman to carry for herself is not my obligation to pick up and carry for her. Sisterhood can call me to care, encourage, support, and sometimes help — but it does not require me to assume responsibility for another woman's life.

So yes, Sis.

I'll reach for you.

I'll encourage you.

I'll root for you.

I'll tell you the truth.

I'll help when I reasonably can.

I'll try not to make you pay for what another woman did to me.

And I hope you'll do the same for another Sis.

But I cannot want your life more than you want it.

I cannot do your self-work for you.

And I cannot lose myself trying to keep you.

I can be my sister's keeper while still expecting my sister to participate in keeping herself.

Maybe that's not a lesser form of sisterhood. Maybe that's what healthy sisterhood actually looks like.
